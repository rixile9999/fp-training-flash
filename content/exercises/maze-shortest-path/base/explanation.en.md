Breadth-first search (BFS) visits cells from nearest to farthest: it looks at every cell at distance 1 from S, then every cell at distance 2, and so on. So the distance at the moment G is first taken out is the shortest distance. What guarantees this order is a first-in, first-out queue. Because the cells found first are taken out first, the cells with smaller distances are always at the front.

To build a queue from immutable lists, use two lists: `front` (in the order to take out) and `back` (in reverse order of insertion). Prepend new cells to `back`, and when `front` is empty, reverse `back` once and move it over. Each cell is put in once and moved once, so all queue operations together are O(V) (`amortized-analysis`). Put visited cells in a `Set` and mark them **when they are put into the queue**, so the same cell does not enter the queue several times.

A common mistake is to prepend new cells to the front of the queue. Then the queue becomes a stack and the search becomes depth-first, and there is no longer any guarantee that the first route reaching G is the shortest. The difference shows up clearly in a large room with no walls. Another common mistake is checking visited cells with `list.contains`. It scans the whole list for every cell, which makes it O(V^2), so on an 80x80 grid it is dozens of times slower than the reference solution (`cost-model-immutable-structures`).

Appending to the end of the queue, as in `list.append(rest, next)`, also gives the right answer. On a grid the queue only holds about a grid width's worth of cells at a time, so the difference is small, but in a graph where one cell leads to a huge number of cells, copying the whole queue every time makes it O(V^2). The two-list queue is amortized O(1) per operation no matter what the graph looks like (`amortized-analysis`).
